## The frame — settled, and it governs every pitch

**The guest speaks from outside their own life.** They know everything that happened to them, including how it ended, and they know what was said and written about them since. They answer **as themselves** — in their own register, with their own knowledge of their own actions. No modern vocabulary, no scholarly framing, no citing of sources.

**The host carries posterity; the guest answers from experience.** The host puts the later claim — *"the story goes you were smuggled in rolled up in a carpet"* — and the guest says what actually happened. The guest never has to sound like a historian, and never quotes a source at the audience.

This is not a stylistic preference. Three things depend on it:

- **The show's premise requires it.** "History Answers Back" means answering a charge, and a guest sealed inside their own moment cannot answer one — they do not know it was made. The cold-open hook (*"has been called a seductress for two thousand years… tonight she answers for herself"*) only works because she knows what those two thousand years said.
- **It removes a formula risk.** The rejected alternative was to interview each guest *at* a moment, with events arriving during the conversation and a clock running. That reads well once and becomes a gimmick by the fourth episode, because it pushes every guest toward the edge of their own downfall and locks out the back half of a life.
- **It is the more honest framing.** A simulated live historical moment edges toward pretending the event is happening. A figure reflecting is transparently a device, which is consistent with the disclosure card rather than in tension with it.

**Do not pitch a "when".** There is no moment to choose. If a pitch only works because the guest is caught at a particular hour of a particular day, it is the wrong pitch.

- **Three-part arcs** — every arc is exactly two parts (below).
- **Recognition Tier C pitches, or fewer than three Tier A** — a good episode nobody clicks.
- **Portraying anyone who died less than 100 years ago** — the recency gate is hard, not a judgement call.

  old structure, hook and a next-part title, and was redone.

---

Strictly pitch exactly 3 to 5 high-stakes historical figure or eyewitness scenarios.
For each pitch:
1. Category: Major Historical Figure OR Historical Eyewitness (for sensitive eras).
2. Era & Conflict: High-stakes historical setting.
3. The Modern Controversy: What moral compromise does the Host press on?
4. The Historical Defense: How does the guest justify their actions using their era's worldview without modern apologetics? Build this from documented actions, primary sources, and academically accepted historical consensus about the figure's character and circumstances — not only from surviving verbatim quotations, which for many figures (Cleopatra, for example) are scarce or nonexistent. Grounded reconstruction is expected; invention contradicting the historical record is not.
5. **Recency gate — check this before anything else, and it is a hard gate, not a judgement call.**

   - **Died 100+ years ago** → the figure may be portrayed directly as the interviewed guest, subject to the Likeness Tier rules below.
   - **Died less than 100 years ago, or may still be living** → **never portrayed, never voiced, never named as the guest.** Route to an Eyewitness Substitution, and make the substitute a Fully Anonymous Composite — an invented aide, soldier, clerk or neighbour — not a named real minor associate. The composite may discuss the real figure's documented actions; it may not be them.
   - **Living people** are never depicted, voiced, or cast in any role, including B-roll extras.

   **Why the disclaimer does not cover this.** The disclosure card, the sources and the AI-use toggle address *deception* — whether a viewer could mistake the reconstruction for a recording. They do not address *rights*. Personality and publicity rights survive death in a number of jurisdictions, commonly for 50 to 70 years, and several jurisdictions now regulate synthetic depictions of real people directly. Those are a different problem from honesty, and a disclaimer is not a defence to them. The 100-year floor is set deliberately above the longest common post-mortem window so the question never has to be argued. Anything near the line is not a pitch to argue for — it is a pitch to drop, or a lawyer's question before a single frame is generated.

   This costs the show almost nothing. The casting sweet spot identified below — globally famous, pre-photographic — sits centuries clear of the gate. The gate only ever bites on twentieth-century figures, who were already being routed to Eyewitness Substitution by the Likeness Tier rules for a different reason.


**Every arc is exactly two parts.** Pitch it that way: Part 1 is the inciting conflict and the
compromise, Part 2 is the reckoning. **Do not propose a three-part arc**, and do not describe a
guest as having "enough material for three" — the middle part of a three-part arc is the weakest
position in the series, nobody enters there, many abandon it, and it costs the same to generate as
the parts people watch. Mode 3 states the same rule; it is fixed for the series, not decided per
guest.

6. Likeness Tier — classify as exactly one of:
   - Fully Anonymous Composite: an invented character not claiming to be any specific identifiable person, even when built from real testimony or documented events. Safe for direct AI Cast Generation in Mode 2.
   - Real Identified Figure (Standard): a specific, named real historical person who is not visually ubiquitous. Mode 2 attempts AI Cast Generation first, falling back to the Archival Asset Brief if blocked.
   - Real Identified Figure (Iconic — Reconstructable): a specific, named real figure whose famous pop-culture image is substantially a modern artistic or cinematic invention rather than a documented-from-life likeness — typically pre-modern/ancient figures without reliable life portraiture (Cleopatra, Mark Antony, Alexander the Great, Julius Caesar, and similar). Mode 2 attempts direct AI Cast Generation using a deliberately historically-grounded reconstruction (coin portraiture, contemporary written description, archaeological evidence) that avoids the recognizable modern/cinematic image cluster — falling back to an Eyewitness Substitution only if this reconstruction is also blocked.
   - Real Identified Figure (Iconic): a specific, named real historical person whose famous image IS a genuine documented-from-life likeness with a dense visual record (portraits painted from life, photographs, death masks — e.g., Napoleon, Oppenheimer, Churchill). No historically-grounded alternative exists that would look meaningfully different from the blocked image, so Mode 2 skips direct casting entirely and goes straight to an Eyewitness Substitution: a proximate composite character (an aide, general, rival, family member, physician, or contemporary) who is not visually iconic, through whom the Host presses on the same modern controversy tied to the real figure's actions and words. Tag the Eyewitness Substitution itself as Fully Anonymous Composite (or Real Identified Figure - Standard, if it names a specific minor real associate) for Mode 2 purposes.
Classification guard: heavy reproduction in film and popular art does NOT by itself make a figure plain Iconic tier — that is a common misclassification. The deciding question is always whether the famous image comes from a confirmed life-portrait (life-sitting painting, photograph, death mask) or from modern/cinematic invention over a thinner, divergent real record. Pre-photographic figures default to Reconstructable unless a genuine life-portrait actually exists for them; don't downgrade a figure to plain Iconic — or default them to off-screen/Eyewitness-only — just because they are visually ubiquitous in movies and art.
7. Recognition Tier — how well a general audience knows the name, classified as exactly one of:
   - **A — needs no introduction.** A name a non-specialist recognises unprompted, from school, film or general culture. Cleopatra, Julius Caesar, Alexander, Genghis Khan, Hannibal, Nero, Attila, Joan of Arc, Marco Polo, Vlad III, Montezuma, Blackbeard, Rasputin.
   - **B — known with one clause of context.** Recognised by anyone interested in the era, but a browsing viewer needs "the general who…" to place them.
   - **C — specialist.** Requires real explanation before the premise lands.

   **Test it by writing the thumbnail title.** If the title needs a subtitle explaining who the person is, it is not Tier A.

Constraint: **At least three of the five pitches must be Recognition Tier A, and Tier C is never pitched.** Until the series has an established audience, pitch Tier A only. A compelling moral dilemma attached to a name nobody recognises is a good episode nobody clicks, and this mode will drift toward obscure figures if left alone — obscure lives often have the tidier documented dilemma, which is exactly the trap.

Where the two tier systems agree: the casting sweet spot is a **globally famous pre-photographic figure**. Recognition is highest for the famous names, and the Likeness Tier guard routes pre-photographic figures to Reconstructable, which Mode 2 can cast directly. It is the photographic-era icons — Napoleon, Churchill, Oppenheimer — where fame forces an Eyewitness Substitution and the guest is no longer the person on the thumbnail. Prefer the ancient and medieval world for that reason, not only for the drama.

8. **Story potential — the material the script will need, checked now.** Added 2026-09-22 (Mode 3,
   *The story engine*). A pitch that cannot fill these is a weak episode however famous the name:
   - **The central question** of the arc, in one line a viewer would click on.
   - **At least three myth-vs-record reversals** — things a general viewer "knows" that the sources
     contradict, each with the source that does it.
   - **At least three concrete images** from the sources — a physical object, place or moment, not an
     idea.
   - **The crack** — one moment in the record where this person's composure plausibly cost them
     something.
   Score each pitch on these; rank the pitches by them after the recency gate and the tiers.

## Eyewitness episodes — when the guest is a composite

The recency gate and the Likeness Tier both route modern subjects here, and this is the mode most of the twentieth century will be told in. A composite guest is not a downgrade — a soldier at Kursk describing the battle, or a mid-level party official describing the Chernobyl response, can carry an episode better than the commander or the General Secretary, because a functionary has to answer for what he personally did rather than for policy.

**Recognition Tier applies to the subject, not to the guest.** This is the exception to the Tier A rule above. "A German infantryman" is not a recognisable name and never will be — but *Stalingrad*, *D-Day*, *Chernobyl*, *the Blitz* are Tier A subjects that need no introduction. For an eyewitness episode, run the Recognition Tier test on the **event**, and write the pitch title around it: "A German Soldier at Stalingrad", "A Party Official on the Chernobyl Response". If the event is not Tier A either, the pitch fails the constraint like any other.

**The composite must not resolve to one real person.** The test: could a viewer, or a surviving family member, point at a specific individual and say that is him? "A German infantryman in the 6th Army" passes. "The radio operator of the 3rd company who survived the encirclement" does not — if the description narrows to one identifiable man, it is no longer a composite and the recency gate applies to him. Generalise the rank, the unit and the posting until it does not resolve.

**Sourcing works differently, and this is a strength.** A named figure is built from what that person documentedly did. A composite is assembled from aggregate testimony — oral history collections, letters, diaries, interrogation and trial records, war-diary entries. The kit should name which body of testimony the character is assembled from, and the disclosure can say so plainly: this character is a composite drawn from these accounts. That is a more honest and more defensible claim than a named individual's reconstructed interior life, not a weaker one.

**The register that works for antiquity does not transfer — use the testimony register instead.**

Cleopatra defends her *decisions*: she chose the alliance, and "level, unapologetic, never pleading" is the honest register for a monarch defending statecraft. A conscript did not choose the war. Applying the same register to him produces apologia, because an unapologetic composed defence of 1943 is a different object from an unapologetic defence of 31 BC.

For composite guests the engine changes from **justification** to **testimony**. Five rules follow from that:

- **The host presses on knowledge, not morality.** Not "how do you justify it" — the character can only answer that with invented remorse or invented conviction, and both are fabrications. Ask instead what he saw, when he understood it, what he told himself at the time. Historians have written extensively on exactly that question, so it is answerable from the record.
- **Let the record contradict the character.** The host cites what is documented; the character gives the contemporary rationalisation; the gap between them is the content. The viewer watches the self-deception rather than being asked to accept it. This makes the host *more* present and more factually armed than in a named-figure episode, not less.
- **The character does not get the last word.** Cleopatra closes her own episode. A morally loaded one closes on the record — a card, a documented fact, or the host. Structural rule, not a preference.
- **Allow uncertainty.** Write the register with hesitation permitted: unsure where the record shows people were unsure, silent where they did not know, caught out where the documentation catches them out. Composure is a choice that suits a queen and flatters a functionary.
- **Where consensus exists about what people in that position knew, the character may not claim an ignorance the record does not support.** Invented remorse is a fabrication; invented ignorance is the more dangerous one.

**Not every subject has an eligible composite.** Draw composites from roles whose documented range genuinely includes constraint, confusion and partial knowledge — a conscript, a clerk, a nurse, a villager, a junior official. Do not build one from a role whose defining activity was the atrocity itself. A camp guard has no version of this format that is honest, and the answer there is that the episode is not made, not that the register is softened.

**Packaging is where apologia actually gets read.** The title and thumbnail foreground the event, never the character's defence. "A GERMAN SOLDIER TELLS THE TRUTH" is the failure mode; "STALINGRAD, FROM INSIDE THE POCKET" is not.

**Living named people.** A composite may discuss the documented actions of real named figures — that is ordinary journalism. It may not allege things about them the record does not support, and any such figure who is living or recently dead is discussed only from the documented record, never voiced, never depicted.

Constraint: Do not exceed 5 pitches.
Constraint: Real Identified Figure (Standard) and (Iconic — Reconstructable) pitches use the real person's real name in the pitch title — naming them is the point of the show. For plain Iconic-tier subjects, the pitch title names both the iconic figure (as the subject under discussion) and the eyewitness role (as the actual on-screen guest) — e.g., "Napoleon's Chief of Staff on the Retreat from Russia."
