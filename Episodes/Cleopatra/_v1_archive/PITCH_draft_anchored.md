## Era and conflict

The last generation of the Roman Republic, 51–30 BC. Egypt is the richest kingdom left in the
Mediterranean and has no army that can stand against Rome; Rome is tearing itself apart in civil war
and needs Egypt's grain and gold. Every Roman strongman who wins needs her treasury — and every one who
loses takes his allies down with him. She backs Caesar, then Antony, and loses everything at Actium
and Alexandria to Octavian, who becomes Augustus.

## The charge — what the host presses on

**"Seductress."** Rome's victors wrote her as a foreign queen who captured two Roman generals with sex
and luxury, turned a Roman against Rome, and then abandoned him in the middle of the battle. That is
the Augustan account — the war was declared on her, not on Antony (Cassius Dio 50.4; Plutarch
*Antony* 60), and the Augustan poets fixed the image (Horace *Odes* 1.37, *fatale monstrum*;
Propertius 3.11). It is still the one thing most people know about her.

The modern moral question the host presses underneath it: **statecraft or surrender?** She kept Egypt
independent for twenty-one years by making it necessary to whichever Roman was strongest — through
alliance, children and, at the end, a war. Was that the only way a kingdom without an army survived
Rome, or was it the price at which Egypt's freedom was sold, one Roman at a time? And at the end, did
she betray the man she allied with, or did she refuse to let Rome finish writing her?

## Her defence — in her era's terms, no modern apologetics

- **A monarch's first duty is the kingdom's survival.** A Ptolemy who did not secure Rome's strongest
  man was annexed; her father borrowed ruinously to buy Roman recognition, and the Senate had already
  debated taking Egypt. Alliance with Caesar and Antony was policy, and the children were policy too.
  She will not deny it and will not apologise for it.
- **Rome's story is a Roman civil war with a foreigner painted over it.** Octavian could not triumph
  over Romans, so he declared war on her. The "seductress" is his word, and she knows who wrote it.
- **She was a working ruler, not a courtesan** — the first of her dynasty to speak Egyptian (Plutarch
  *Antony* 27), crowned in the Egyptian rites, managing grain, temples and a divided population.
- **At the end she chose her death over Octavian's triumph.** Built from documented action, not from
  surviving speech: verbatim words of hers are almost nonexistent, so her voice is grounded
  reconstruction from what she did and what the sources report of her.

Register (from `CAST.md`): composed and unapologetic, corrects premises rather than defending herself,
never pleads, formal — no contractions. Anger held under; never raised, never breaks.

## The arc — exactly two parts

**Central question of the arc:** *Statecraft or surrender — and who gets to write the answer, her or
Rome?*

### Part 1 of 2 — the inciting conflict and the compromise (settled; rebuilt by Mode 3)
*Working title direction: the hook line — **"Egypt did not survive without me."***

What she was handed (a rich kingdom with no army, a father's debts, a Senate eyeing annexation, a
brother's court that expels her) → **Caesar** (Pompey's head at the shoreline; the bedding sack, not
the carpet; the winter besieged in the palace quarter; the son she names for him; his murder) →
**Antony** (a decade, three children, the Donations of Alexandria; Octavian turns it into the charge;
the will read in the Senate; the war declared on her) → **Actium**: sixty ships, hers, through a gap in
the line, sailing south in the middle of the battle.

Ends on the strongest unanswered question: **what did she mean by the sixty ships** — and the host's
*"Was that what it was?"* about Antony, answered *"A treaty first."* The part's question (statecraft or
surrender) is put to her directly at the end of the Caesar act and answered as a thesis.

### Part 2 of 2 — the reckoning
*Working title direction: **"Actium, and the Sixty Ships"*** (already promised by Part 1's end card,
`card_data_p1.json`).

Part 2 pays exactly what Part 1 promised — **the sixty ships first** — then climbs to the end:

1. **Actium.** Flight or breakout? She answers the question the host promised to ask *properly*.
2. **The year between** (31–30 BC). Octavian's secret offers to her to give up Antony; **Pelusium**
   surrendered to Octavian, with her consent according to rumour (Plutarch *Antony* 74) and on her
   orders according to Dio (51.9) — while she handed the commander's wife and children to Antony to be
   killed. The betrayal charge at its sharpest.
3. **Antony's death.** Hauled up into her monument on ropes, dying there (Plutarch *Antony* 77). The
   host says the obvious thing — *you loved him* — so she can refuse it. The treaty question from
   Part 1 gets its answer here, or its refusal.
4. **Octavian.** The victor's interview in Alexandria; the news that she will be taken to Rome to walk
   in his triumph.
5. **Her death, and her son.** *"I will not be led in triumph"* (Livy, preserved in a commentary on
   Horace). Caesarion — the boy she called *"part of that"* in Part 1 — killed on Octavian's order after
   her death (*"too many Caesars"*). She knows; she speaks from outside her life.
6. **The last word is hers.** She is a named figure defending her own decisions, not a composite — so
   she closes her own episode, and the close turns the charge back: Rome wrote the ending, and even
   Rome's own historians could not say how she died.

**Promise → payoff:** Part 1's cold open promises *"tonight she answers for herself"* against
*"seductress"*; Part 1 pays it on Rome's side of the story (the charge was Octavian's), Part 2 pays it
on hers (the one decision no Roman could make for her).

## Handling notes for Modes 3–6

- **Suicide in Part 2.** Historical death, discussed, never depicted: no method shown in b-roll, no
  snake on screen, no lingering on the body. The figs basket and the diadem carry it. Keep the title
  and thumbnail on Actium and the charge, not on her death. (YouTube treats educational historical
  discussion as acceptable; graphic depiction is what to avoid.)
- **Seleucus's family** — killings attributed to her by a hostile source; the host puts it, she answers
  it, and the record's disagreement is stated. Not softened, not asserted as fact.
- **The library line in v1** (*"That version of the story grew later, and it grew in Rome"*) overstates:
  Plutarch, who says the great library burned (*Caesar* 49), wrote in Greek. Mode 3 should rewrite the
  tail of that line, not carry it in as is.
- **"Nine languages"** — Plutarch *Antony* 27 lists the peoples she spoke with without an interpreter;
  Mode 3 should confirm the count before it is a `[D]` line.
