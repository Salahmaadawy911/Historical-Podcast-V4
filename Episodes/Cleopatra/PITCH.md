## 2 · Era and conflict

The last generation of the Roman Republic. Egypt is the richest kingdom left in the Mediterranean and
has no army that can stand against Rome; Rome is in civil war and needs Egypt's grain and money. Every
Roman who wins needs her treasury, and every Roman who loses drags his allies down with him. She allies
with Caesar, then Antony, and loses the kingdom to Octavian, who becomes Augustus.

## 3 · The charge — carried in as approved

**"Seductress."** The victors' account: a foreign queen who captured two Roman generals with sex and
luxury, turned a Roman against Rome, and ran in the middle of the battle. Rome declared the war on her,
not on Antony — *"a vote was passed to wage war against Cleopatra, and to take away from Antony the
authority which he had surrendered to a woman"* (Plutarch *Antony* 60, **verified**); Dio says the Senate
avoided naming Antony an enemy so that his supporters could desert him as patriots (50.4–6, **verified**).
The Augustan poets fixed the image — Horace's *fatale monstrum* (*Odes* 1.37, **verified**).

**The moral question underneath, which the host presses:** statecraft or surrender. She kept Egypt a
kingdom for twenty-one years by making it necessary to whichever Roman was strongest — through alliance,
children and at the end a war. Was that the only way a kingdom without an army outlasted Rome, or the
price at which Egypt's freedom was sold, one Roman at a time?

## 4 · Her defence — in her era's terms

- **A monarch's first duty is the kingdom's survival.** A Ptolemy without Rome's strongest man behind
  him was annexed; her father bought Roman recognition with ruinous debt. Alliance was policy, and the
  children were policy. She neither denies it nor apologises for it.
- **The charge is a Roman civil war with a foreign woman painted over it.** A Roman could not triumph
  over Romans; he could over her. She knows who chose the word.
- **She was a working ruler.** The first of her line to learn Egyptian — *"the kings of Egypt before her
  had not even made an effort to learn the native language"* (Plutarch *Antony* 27, **verified
  2026-09-23**).
- **At the end she chose her death over Rome's triumph.**
- Built from documented action: verbatim words of hers barely survive, so her voice is grounded
  reconstruction, never invention against the record.

## 5 · The arc — exactly two parts

**Central question of the arc:** *Did she seduce Rome — or hold it off for twenty years with the only
weapons Egypt had left? And who gets to write the answer?*

The two parts below are **questions and the documented record**, not an act plan. Events are listed in
historical order so Mode 3 can see what exists; it chooses the order, the emphasis and the break.

### Part 1 of 2 — the inciting conflict and the compromise

**Part question:** statecraft or surrender?

**Questions the part has to put to her** (the ladder runs biography → decision → cost → charge):
- What was she actually handed?
- Why Caesar — and what did the alliance cost her own family?
- Why Antony — and what did she mean by making her children kings?
- Is the charge true?

**The documented record available:**
- Her father's reign: Roman recognition bought with debt; a shared throne with her brother; expelled by
  his court.
- 48 BC: Pompey killed on the shore at Pelusium; his head brought to Caesar, who *"turned away in horror"*
  from it but took Pompey's seal-ring *"and shed tears over it"* (Plutarch *Caesar* 48, **verified**).
- Her entry to the palace in a bed-sack that Apollodorus *"tied up with a cord and carried indoors to
  Caesar"* (Plutarch *Caesar* 49, **verified**).
- The Alexandrian War: besieged in the palace quarter through the winter of 48–47; fire at the
  harbour.
- Her sister **Arsinoe** proclaimed queen against her, captured, and led in chains in Caesar's triumph
  in Rome, 46 BC — the sight *"aroused very great pity"* (Dio 43.19, **verified**). Cleopatra was living
  in Caesar's house in Rome at the time (Dio 43.27, **verified**).
- Caesarion. The Ides of March, 44 BC; she leaves Rome.
- 41 BC, Tarsus: the barge on the Cydnus — *"gilded poop… sails spread purple… silver oars"*, she
  *"adorned like Venus in a painting"* (Plutarch *Antony* 26, **verified**). Staged, public, political.
- Arsinoe killed as a suppliant in a temple of Artemis, by Antony's assassins at Cleopatra's wish — at
  **Ephesus** in Josephus (*AJ* 15.89, **verified**), at **Miletus** (Artemis Leucophryne) in Appian (*BC*
  5.9, **verified**). The sources disagree on the place: say *"a temple of Artemis"*, never a city, unless the
  disagreement is the point.
- A decade with Antony; three children. 34 BC, the Donations of Alexandria: the gymnasium, *"a tribunal
  of silver"* and *"two thrones of gold"*; she is declared Queen of Egypt, Cyprus, Libya and Coele Syria,
  sharing the throne with Caesarion; their sons are proclaimed *"Kings of Kings"* (Plutarch *Antony* 54,
  **verified**).
- Antony's will: Octavian took it from the Vestal Virgins, marked passages, and read them to the Senate —
  chiefly that Antony's body be sent to Cleopatra in Egypt (Plutarch *Antony* 58, **verified**). Plutarch
  does not doubt the will; **modern historians do** (that it was genuine is contested — cite a modern
  source in Mode 3 if the line says so). War declared on her, 32 BC.
- **Actium, 31 BC — the hinge.** It can close Part 1 or open Part 2; Mode 3 decides by the strongest
  unanswered question, not by the calendar.

### Part 2 of 2 — the reckoning

**Part question:** *When there was nothing left to bargain with, what did she still decide — and for
whom?*

**Questions the part has to put to her** (cost → charge → the thing she will not say):
- Did she run at Actium?
- Did she sell Antony to save herself?
- What was he to her?
- Why die — and why then?
- What happened to her son, and did she know it would?

**The documented record available:**
- Actium: Antony himself ordered the sails carried aboard, *"saying that not one fugitive of the enemy
  should be allowed to make his escape"* (Plutarch *Antony* 64, **verified**); *"the sixty ships of
  Cleopatra were seen hoisting their sails for flight and making off through the midst of the
  combatants"* (*Antony* 66, **verified**).
- The year between, 31–30 BC: embassies to Octavian.
- Pelusium falls: *"a rumour that Seleucus had given it up, and not without the consent of Cleopatra"* —
  and she *"allowed Antony to put to death the wife and children of Seleucus"* (Plutarch *Antony* 74,
  **verified**). Dio is certain: *"ostensibly by storm, but really because it was betrayed by Cleopatra"*
  (51.9, **verified**).
- Antony's death: fastened to ropes and drawn up into her monument — *"with clinging hands and strained
  face"* (Plutarch *Antony* 77, **verified**).
- Octavian in Alexandria; Dolabella warns her that she and her children are to be sent off *"within three
  days"* (Plutarch *Antony* 84, **verified**).
- Her death: the countryman with the basket of figs; Charmion, dying, arranging the diadem — *"befitting
  the descendant of so many kings"* (Plutarch *Antony* 85, **verified**). How she died was not known: Strabo,
  the earliest, gives *"the bite of an asp or… a poisonous ointment"* (17.1.10); Plutarch lists asp, or poison
  in a hollow comb, and says the only signs were faint pricks on the arm (*Antony* 86); Dio: *"No one knows
  clearly in what way she perished"* — asp, or a poisoned hairpin (51.14). All **verified**. The asp became
  the story through the image of her *"with the asp clinging to her"* carried in Octavian's triumph
  (*Antony* 86, **verified**). She died at 39, after 22 years as queen (*Antony* 86).
- Caesarion, sent south towards India with treasure, persuaded back by his tutor Rhodon, and killed
  *"after the death of Cleopatra"* — Areius's *"Not a good thing were a Caesar too many"* (Plutarch *Antony*
  81–82, **verified**). Dio has him overtaken on the road to Ethiopia and murdered (51.15, **verified**) — the
  sources differ on how, not on what. Her children by Antony *"reared"* by Octavia with her own (*Antony*
  87, **verified**). Egypt becomes Rome's.
- **The last word is hers.** She is a named figure defending her own decisions, not a composite.

## 6 · Story potential (pitch item 8)

**Reversals — what a viewer "knows" against what the sources say.** At least three per part are
available; Mode 3 picks one per act.

| Myth | Record | Status |
|---|---|---|
| She won Rome with her beauty | Plutarch: her beauty *"was in itself not altogether incomparable, nor such as to strike those who saw her"*; the charm was her talk and voice (*Antony* 27) | **verified** |
| She was Egyptian | A Macedonian Greek dynasty — and she was its first ruler to learn Egyptian (*Antony* 27) | **verified** |
| Rolled out of a carpet | A bed-sack, tied with a cord (*Caesar* 49) | **verified** |
| Caesar burned the Library of Alexandria | **Weak — use with care.** Both ancient sources say the fire from the docks reached the library (*Caesar* 49; Dio 42.38). The doubt is modern: how much burned, and whether it was the Library itself or books stored near the harbour | sources **verified**; the modern doubt needs a modern citation in Mode 3 |
| Rome went to war with Antony | Rome declared war on *her* (*Antony* 60; Dio 50.4–6) | **verified** |
| She panicked at Actium | Antony ordered the sails aboard; Plutarch gives pursuit as his reason, and modern historians read it as a planned breakout — the record itself is open | **verified** (Plutarch); historians' view to cite in Mode 3 |
| She sold Antony out | Pelusium is *rumour* in Plutarch (*Antony* 74), *betrayal* in Dio (51.9) — the sources disagree | **verified** |
| She died of grief for Antony | She outlived him and died once she learned she was to be sent off within three days (*Antony* 84) | **verified** |
| The asp | Nobody knew how she died; the snake became fact through the image in Octavian's triumph (Strabo 17.1.10; *Antony* 86; Dio 51.14) | **verified** |

**Concrete images** (all are also b-roll candidates):
Pompey's head on the shore · the bedding sack · the harbour on fire · **Arsinoe in chains in a Roman
triumph** · **the barge on the Cydnus**, purple sails and silver oars · the Donations: thrones in the
gymnasium · sixty ships under sail through the line · **the ropes at the monument** · the basket of figs
· Charmion and the diadem · the effigy with the snake, carried through Rome.

**The crack — one per part, candidates only; Mode 3 chooses:**
- **Part 1 — Arsinoe.** Her sister walked in chains in a Roman triumph while she lived in Caesar's
  house, and was later killed at her request. It is the family cost of the Caesar alliance, it is a
  charge she cannot correct as a premise, and it sets up Part 2: the triumph is the thing she will
  later die rather than walk in.
- **Part 2 — Caesarion.** The son she made Caesar's heir, killed because of that name. She speaks from
  outside her life, so she knows. Alternative: the monument — Antony drawn up on ropes.

**Score:** all four items met in both parts. Part 1 is stronger on images (Cydnus, Arsinoe), Part 2 on
reversals. Part 1's risk is a slow background opening — the lesson below applies.

## 7 · Handling notes for Modes 3–6

- **Suicide (Part 2).** Historical death, discussed, never depicted: no method in b-roll, no snake on
  screen, no lingering on the body. The figs and the diadem carry it. Titles and thumbnails stay on the
  charge, not on her death.
- **Killings attributed to her** — Arsinoe; Seleucus's family; her brother Ptolemy XIV, whom Josephus says
  she poisoned *"when he was but fifteen years old"* (*AJ* 15.89, **verified** — a hostile source, writing for
  a Jewish audience against her). The host puts each; she answers; where the sources disagree, that is
  said. Not softened, not asserted as fact.
- **The Library.** Both Plutarch and Dio — Greek writers — say the library burned. Don't frame the burning
  as a Roman invention, and don't make it a myth the record contradicts: the record reports it; only its
  scale is doubted, by modern scholars.
- **Her languages.** Plutarch names **seven** peoples she answered without an interpreter — Ethiopians,
  Troglodytes, Hebrews, Arabians, Syrians, Medes, Parthians — and says *"many others"* (*Antony* 27,
  **verified**). "Nine languages" is not in the source; don't use a number beyond the seven named.
- **Actium.** Plutarch's own stated reason for the sails is pursuit. A planned breakout is a modern
  reading — give it to her as her claim `[I]`, or state that historians are divided; never as `[D]`.

## 8 · Carry-ins for Mode 3 — facts and principles only

**Facts and corrections** (from the archive, each re-verified where it becomes a `[D]` line):
- bedding sack, not carpet (*Caesar* 49)
- the Library: the ancient sources report the fire reached it; only modern scholars doubt the scale
- the palace siege lasted about a winter (48–47 BC) — give no month count
- Octavian took **Pelusium**; do not say he "landed in the delta"
- Antony's will: Plutarch reports it without doubt; its authenticity is a modern question
- Arsinoe's death: Ephesus (Josephus) or Miletus (Appian) — say "a temple of Artemis"
- Caesarion's death: lured back (Plutarch) or caught on the road (Dio)
- "seven peoples named", not "nine languages"

**Pronunciation** (from the archive): Ptolemaic *tol-uh-MAY-ik* · Parthia *PAR-thee-uh* · Octavian
*ok-TAY-vee-un* · Actium *AK-tee-um*. New names this arc may need: Pelusium, Caesarion, Arsinoe,
Charmion, Iras, Dolabella, Seleucus, Cydnus, Apollodorus — `lines_check.py` will list the ones the
outline uses.

**Sources:** the curated list in `card_data_p1.json`, re-audited by Mode 4. Add Strabo, Appian and
