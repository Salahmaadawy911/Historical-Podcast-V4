Reels are not content. They are the doorway to the episode. Every decision in this
mode is judged by one question: does this make someone open the full video?

## Output

For each episode, produce exactly **three** vertical cutdowns — one promo and two moments.

1. **The Promo Trailer** — released *before* the episode goes live, 30–45s.
   A highlights cut: the host's opening charge, two or three of the guest's sharpest
   returns, and the question the episode turns on left unanswered. It is a trailer,
   so it withholds the answer by construction. Ends on the release date and the
   episode title.
2. **Moment Reel A** — released with or just after the episode, 30–60s.
   One self-contained exchange: a single quote or a single question that lands on its
   own with no setup. Not a highlights montage — one moment.
3. **Moment Reel B** — released a few days later, 30–60s.
   A *different* moment, from a different act, in a different emotional register from A.
   If A is the guest being sharp, B is the host conceding, or the guest being dry, or a
   fact that surprises. Never the same beat recut.

Three per episode is the ceiling, not a target to pad toward. At a fortnightly episode
that is roughly one and a half a week.

## Rules

1. **The vertical format: the clip sits on a paper page.** Do not pan-and-scan a 16:9 frame into 9:16 — the guest sits screen-right and the host screen-left, so a centre crop cuts through whichever one is talking, and a tracking crop turns a locked interview into a moving one.

   Instead: place the **uncropped 16:9 clip in the middle of a 9:16 paper frame**, with `paper_source.png` filling the bands above and below. The statement sits on the band above, the mark on the band below. Nothing is cropped, nothing moves, and the reel is recognisable as this channel in the first frame.

   | | |
   |---|---|
   | Ground | `paper_source.png`, full frame |
   | Clip | uncropped 16:9, full width, vertically centred |
   | Statement | above the clip, Anton caps in ink, from the part's Key Lines |
   | Subtitles | **burned, always** — most of this audience watches muted. Ink on the lower paper band, never over the picture |
   | Mark | `brand_mark_trimmed.png` in ink, bottom |

   This is the same system as the thumbnail and the plates: **paper ground, ink type, one warm accent.** A reel that does not look like it came from this channel is doing half its job, since driving viewers to the main video is the entire point.

2. **Cut from the clean master.** The published part carries burned subtitles, lower thirds, pull-quotes, context cards and source attributions, all positioned for 16:9. **A two-up stacks vertically in a reel** — host above, guest below, each cropped from its own clip; never a crop of the 16:9 two-up. **Context cards are cut off by a vertical crop** — they sit at the frame edge by design. A reel that needs one gets its own vertical treatment (rule 1) from the same §12 text, never a crop of the 16:9 card. A vertical crop slices straight through them. Mode 6 finishes every part twice — a clean master with no text of any kind, and the titled master that gets published. Reels come from the clean one and get the vertical treatment in rule 1. Cutting from the titled master is a mistake that stays invisible until the reel is already vertical.

3. **Source Constraint.** A Reel is cut from clips the episode already generated — never
   regenerated at 9:16, never re-prompted for a vertical framing. A regenerated clip is a
   fresh take of the voice, and a Reel that sounds like a different person from the episode
   it advertises undoes the consistency work. Vertical framing is achieved by pan-and-scan
   on the existing 16:9 clip. Reels therefore cost zero generation credits.

4. **Never post the same cut twice.** Each Reel is a distinct moment with its own hook,
   its own framing, and its own text treatment. Reposting one cut with a new caption, or
   slicing one exchange into near-identical variants, is the single behaviour most likely
   to draw a channel-level inauthentic-content judgement — high volume, low variation, one
   source. The episodes are defensible on their craft; a firehose of near-identical Shorts
   is not, and the judgement lands on the whole channel.

5. **Vary the surface.** Across an arc, no two Reels should share a framing, an opening
   frame, a text position, or a thumbnail treatment. A fixed studio format quietly pushes
   everything toward one template — resist it deliberately.

6. **Disclosure Bumper.** A 2–3 second text overlay at the start of each Reel carrying the
   short version of the episode's trust disclaimer. Reels circulate out of context far more
   than full episodes, so this cannot be dropped for pacing. Flip the "altered or synthetic
   content" toggle in Studio on every upload, Reels included.

7. **Call to the episode.** Every Reel names the episode and points to it — end card, pinned
   comment, description. Shorts views do not count toward the watch-hour threshold that
   monetisation runs on; their only measurable job is moving a viewer to long-form. A Reel
   that performs well and sends nobody to the episode has failed.

## Per-Reel deliverables

- **Transcript excerpt** — exact, with the source shot IDs it is cut from.
- **Re-framing directives** — pan-and-scan or split-screen moves for 9:16, per shot.
- **Sound & VFX directives** — text pop-ups, sound-effect hits, pacing markers.
- **Hook line** — the first three seconds, written out.
- **Release slot** — before the episode, with it, or days after.
